## 0. Stress-testing the premise (read this first)

### 0.1 Pushback on the framing

1. **"The marginal cost of intelligence is falling toward zero" mixes up two things.** What is getting cheaper is *producing plausible cognitive output*. Three things are not getting cheaper: **verification, accountability and judgment under uncertainty.** Errors in AI output are "jagged" (Dell'Acqua et al. 2023), so they are hard to spot unless you already know the domain. As output gets cheaper, the human ability to evaluate it becomes *more* valuable. The working-life problem is therefore not mainly "people will think less". It is "**we may stop producing the people who can check the machines**".

2. **"Use it or lose it" is true of specific skills, but it is a poor guide to policy.** We have rightly let many skills go: mental arithmetic to calculators, memorising routes to maps, memory in general to writing. Nobody should mourn them. The useful question is not "how do we keep using everything?" but **which capabilities are load-bearing**:
   - **Supervisory:** needed to direct and check AI.
   - **Fallback:** needed when AI fails, is absent, or is wrong at a critical moment.
   - **Constitutive:** valued for themselves, because they make up identity, relationships and meaning.

   Policy should protect these three categories, not cognitive effort for its own sake.

3. **In my life stage, the biggest risk is institutional, not personal.** Adults losing skills through convenience is real but second-order. The first-order risks are structural:
   - **Entry-level jobs disappearing.** These are the "developmental labour market" where expertise was formed.
   - **Workplaces redesigned so that experts only supervise.**
   - **Work shrinking as a social structure** that provides time structure, social contact, purpose and status (Jahoda's "latent functions" of employment).

   A contract built on telling people to "use your brain more" would miss most of this.

4. **For old age, the strongest levers have nothing to do with AI**: hearing, blood pressure, physical activity, education and social contact (Lancet Commission 2024). An "AI and cognition" framing risks pulling attention toward exciting interventions and away from dull, proven ones. My proposals deliberately include the dull ones.

5. **The right role for AI changes across the life course, so no single rule fits everyone.** "Scaffold, don't substitute" is right while skills are being formed. In late life, *compensation* is legitimate and good. This is "selective optimisation with compensation" (Baltes & Baltes 1990). A hearing aid substitutes for a lost function, and that is the point. The line that matters in old age is not substitution versus scaffolding but **whether AI preserves agency and human connection or erodes them.**

### 0.2 Challenging the definition of "human intelligence flourishing"

The six dimensions in the brief are a good start. I propose four amendments.

- **Add metacognition and calibration.** This means knowing what you know, knowing when to trust a tool, and having confidence that matches your accuracy. It is the skill most directly threatened by fluent AI, and the one that supervising AI most depends on. "Judgment" covers it only partly.
- **Add embodied and sensory capacity.** Cognition depends on the body: hearing, vision, mobility and sleep. Untreated hearing loss is among the largest modifiable dementia risk factors [E]. A definition limited to the mind will under-invest in the body.
- **Add collective intelligence.** This means the capacity of teams, professions and democratic institutions to deliberate, disagree productively, and keep diverse viewpoints. AI may raise each individual's output while making output across people more alike (Doshi & Hauser 2024) [C]. The brief mentions "societal level" but none of the six dimensions measures it.
- **Define flourishing as capability, not performance** (Sen/Nussbaum). What matters is what people are *really able* to do and be, including the freedom to choose not to do some things. This guards against paternalism: adults may offload, but they should not be *unable* to do otherwise when it matters.

### 0.3 Steelman: AI will raise human capability

This case is serious and partly supported by evidence.

- **History.** Socrates warned in the *Phaedrus* that writing would ruin memory. Printing and literacy instead built cognitive capacities that were new to most people. Calculator meta-analyses found no harm to paper-and-pencil arithmetic skills when calculators were used alongside instruction (Hembree & Dessart 1986; Ellington 2003) [E].
- **The Flynn effect** showed that *environments* can raise measured intelligence quickly. An environment rich in AI tutors could be a strong cognitive stimulus [X].
- **Tutoring at scale.** In a Harvard physics RCT, students using a well-designed AI tutor learned more in less time than in active-learning classes (Kestin et al. 2025) [C, but strong design]. A short World Bank pilot in Nigeria found sizeable gains in English [C]. These results apply directly to adult reskilling.
- **Novices gain the most.** In customer service, AI raised productivity by about 14% on average and about 34% for novices. Agents who had used the AI kept some of the gain during system outages, which suggests real learning, not just dependence (Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond 2025) [E for productivity, C for learning]. Similar narrowing of skill gaps appears in writing (Noy & Zhang 2023) and consulting (Dell'Acqua et al. 2023) [E].
- **Compensation in late life.** AI can extend independence for people with dyslexia, aphasia, sensory loss or early cognitive decline. It works as a cognitive prosthesis that delays the move into institutional care [X, plausible].
- **Freed-up time.** If routine work is offloaded, time and attention could go to higher-order work, relationships and craft [X; whether this happens depends on institutions].

### 0.4 Where the deterioration risk is real, and where it is speculative

| Claim | Status | Key evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Skills decay with disuse, faster for cognitive than physical skills | **[E]** | Arthur et al. 1998 meta-analysis |
| Automation causes complacency, automation bias and "out-of-the-loop" deskilling | **[E]** | Bainbridge 1983; Parasuraman & Manzey 2010; aviation incident record (AF447, Colgan 3407); FAA SAFO 13002 urging more manual flying |
| Pilots' *cognitive* flying skills (navigation, situational awareness) erode more than stick-and-rudder skills | **[E/C]** | Casner et al. 2014 |
| Heavy GPS use is linked to worse spatial memory (with some longitudinal evidence); deliberate navigation training grows the hippocampus | **[E/C]** | Dahmani & Bohbot 2020; Maguire et al. 2000; Woollett & Maguire 2011 |
| Clinicians' unaided performance falls after routine AI assistance | **[C], emerging and important** | In multicentre colonoscopy data, adenoma detection *without* AI fell from about 28% to 22% after AI was introduced (Budzyń et al., *Lancet Gastroenterol Hepatol* 2025) |
| Wrong AI suggestions pull experts' judgments, with inexperienced readers most affected | **[E/C]** | Dratsch et al. 2023 (mammography) |
| Unguarded generative AI improves practice performance but harms learning | **[C], strong RCT** | Bastani et al. 2025 (PNAS): practice +48%, exams −17%; a version with guardrails removed most of the harm |
| Trusting AI more goes with less critical thinking at work | **[C], self-report** | Lee et al. 2025 (CHI, n=319 knowledge workers); Gerlich 2025 (cross-sectional) |
| Writing essays with LLMs lowers neural engagement and recall of one's own text | **[C], small preprint** | Kosmyna et al. 2025 (n=54) |
| AI raises individual creativity but makes output more alike across people | **[C]** | Doshi & Hauser 2024 |
| Entry-level hiring is falling in AI-exposed jobs | **[C], early and disputed** | Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen 2025: about 13% relative employment decline for 22–25-year-olds in the most exposed occupations (US payroll data). Others find no broad disruption so far (e.g. Yale Budget Lab 2025). Danish register data show close to zero effects of chatbots on earnings and hours so far (Humlum & Vestergaard 2025) |
| Measured intelligence is falling in recent cohorts, for environmental reasons | **[E] for Norway; cause unknown** | Bratsberg & Rogeberg 2018: the decline appears *within families*, so it is environmental. It **began with cohorts born around 1975, before smartphones or AI**, so AI cannot be the original cause |
| Adult literacy is declining | **[E] internationally, not in Norway** | PIAAC 2023: most countries were flat or declining. **Norway held steady in literacy and improved in numeracy.** Finland and Denmark improved significantly |
| AI will cause society-wide cognitive decline | **Speculative** | No direct evidence yet; it is too early |
| A world with little paid work will cause mass loss of meaning | **Speculative** | Depends on labour-market effects that have not appeared yet. Unemployment harms well-being beyond lost income [E], but a well-funded transition is different from unemployment |

**Verdict.** The risk is real but **conditional on how AI is used and on institutional incentives**, not on the technology alone. The same tool can scaffold or substitute (Bastani et al.). That is exactly why this is a *social contract* question. Left alone, firms rationally choose substitution because it pays off this quarter, while the cost (a missing pipeline of experts, a deskilled workforce) falls on other people later. **Deskilling is an externality.**

## 1. Diagnosis

### Five mechanisms of erosion, ages 22 to end of life

**M1. The broken apprenticeship ladder ("the missing rung").** Expertise has always been built on low-stakes, repetitive entry-level work: the first drafts, document reviews, simple code, routine case files. AI now does exactly these tasks.

There are two failure routes:
- **(a) Firms stop hiring juniors.** The expertise pipeline thins and the cost appears 10–15 years later as a shortage of senior judgment [C/X].
- **(b) Juniors are hired but use AI to *perform* without *learning*.** This is the performance–learning paradox [C; Bastani et al.]. They never build the mental models that let experts see when something is wrong.

*Most at risk:* the first 2–5 years of a career, and anyone retraining into a new field at any age.

**M2. Experts slide into supervision.** When AI handles 95% of cases, professionals stop practising the core skill and only review the output. Their skill decays [E] just as the remaining cases become the hardest: edge cases, novel situations, and AI failures. This is Bainbridge's "irony of automation". Supervision itself also degrades because people check less as AI gets more reliable (automation complacency) [E].

*Most at risk:* the move to an "AI orchestrator" role (often around ages 30–40), and roles where serious events are rare.

**M3. Judgment is outsourced and thinking converges.** Here the deeper danger is not offloading the *task* but offloading the *evaluation*. Fluent, confident output leads people to accept answers they cannot check, and confidence drifts away from competence. At the level of society, groups using the same models converge on similar framings, which weakens the disagreement that collective intelligence depends on [C/X].

*Most at risk:* professions whose value is judgment (law, medicine, policy, journalism, management), and public decisions.

**M4. Work weakens as a structure for learning and meaning.** For most adults, work is the main driver of ongoing learning, and it supplies time structure, social contact, collective purpose, status and activity (Jahoda) [E]. AI-driven displacement, fragmented gig work and longer retirements all weaken this structure. Adult learning also shows a strong **Matthew effect**: people with high skills train far more than people with low skills [E, PIAAC and SSB's Lærevilkårsmonitoren]. So disruption pushes those already ahead further ahead, and those with fewer resources fall behind.

*Most at risk:* sudden displacement in mid-career (ages 40–55), long-term sick leave and disability, which are major exit routes in Norway, and leaving work at retirement.

**M5. Late-life withdrawal spiral.** Retirement removes daily cognitive and social demands. Untreated hearing and vision loss reduce social contact. Loneliness rises after bereavement. Then frictionless AI takes over errands, navigation, conversation and planning. Each step reduces stimulation and contact, which speeds decline [E for the risk factors, X for the AI step]. AI companions can *bridge* to human contact or *substitute* for it. Early evidence suggests heavy use is linked to more loneliness and dependence [C; Fang et al. 2025, MIT/OpenAI 4-week RCT]. Meanwhile, when experts retire, their **tacit knowledge** leaves with them unless it was passed on through working alongside others.

*Most at risk:* retirement (62–70), becoming widowed, onset of sensory loss or frailty (75+), and moving into care.

### Key moments of highest risk

```
22–27  Entry       → M1: does the first job teach?
28–40  Promotion   → M2: from doing to reviewing
40–55  Disruption  → M4: displacement, retraining, caring for parents
55–70  Exit        → M4/M5: retirement transition, tacit knowledge leaves
70+    Late life   → M5: sensory loss, bereavement, frailty, AI companions
```

## 2. Design principles

**P1. The role of AI follows the life course: scaffold during formation, sparring partner during mastery, prosthesis during decline.** While a skill is being formed (juniors and career changers alike), AI must make the learner do the thinking first. During mastery, AI should challenge and extend, not replace. In decline, compensation is legitimate. **At every stage, AI should preserve agency and human connection.**

**P2. Keep supervisory and fallback competence where accountability lies.** Wherever a human signs off, that human must be able to do the task, or at least catch its failures, without AI. The required level of unaided proficiency should rise with the stakes, as it does in aviation.

**P3. Those who deplete skills help pay to rebuild them.** Deskilling and the missing junior rung are externalities. Employers that capture productivity gains by removing developmental work should contribute to rebuilding skills. Learning rights belong to the *person* and move with them between jobs.

**P4. Friction by design, choice by default.** AI products should make learning modes easy to find and good, but adults keep the right to offload. Mandates are limited to (a) safety-critical roles and (b) training settings society pays for. People should be nudged, not policed.

**P5. Contribution, not only employment, anchors identity.** Recognise and fund non-market contribution, such as mentoring, care and civic roles, as legitimate sources of meaning and continued learning. This matters most where paid work shrinks and after retirement.

**P6. Proportionate universalism.** Make every measure universal, but put more effort into those least likely to invest in themselves: people with low skills, older workers, people on long-term benefits, and isolated older people. Otherwise cognitive flourishing becomes a luxury good.

## 3. Interventions

Costs are rough orders of magnitude for Norway (about 5.6 million people) **[X]** and need proper costing.

### I-1. The Professional Apprenticeship: *Fagbrev*-style training for knowledge work
*Addresses M1, and connects to I-5 and the pilot.*

- **Actors:** The social partners (NHO/KS/Spekter with LO/YS/Unio/Akademikerne), the state through HK-dir, professional bodies.
- **Mechanism:** Extend Norway's strong vocational apprenticeship model to graduate-entry knowledge jobs. Every participating junior gets a structured two-year contract with:
  - **Protected learning time** (target 20%).
  - **"Attempt first" rules:** the junior produces a first draft or diagnosis before consulting AI.
  - **Regular AI-off practice** on core tasks, with feedback.
  - **A named mentor** whose mentoring hours count as productive time.
  - **A competence sign-off** recognised across employers, a portable "professional *fagbrev*".
- **Who pays:** The state funds a per-apprentice grant modelled on the existing *lærlingtilskudd*. The 2025 basic grant was about NOK 183,000 per full-time year. The grant is conditional on meeting the training standard. Employers cover salary and mentor time. *Example:* 8,000 places × about NOK 180k ≈ **NOK 1.5bn/year**.
- **What makes it stick:** It is written into collective agreements (*tariffavtaler*). It runs through the existing tripartite apprenticeship system rather than a new bureaucracy. Public employers lead through procurement and their own hiring.
- **Evidence:** **[E]** strong returns to apprenticeships in trades (Germany, Switzerland, Norway). **[E]** desirable difficulties and the generation effect in learning science. **[X]** untested for AI-era knowledge work, so it needs a pilot.
- **Main risk:** Deadweight (subsidising juniors firms would hire anyway) and apprentices used as cheap labour. *Mitigation:* condition the grant on *net* additional junior hires and on audited training quality.
- **[Generalises]** In countries with weak social partners, use a tax credit for certified junior-training hours instead.

### I-2. "Manual hours": supervisory competence standards in high-stakes professions
*Addresses M2 and M3.*

- **Actors:** Sector regulators and professional bodies (Helsedirektoratet/Helsetilsynet, Luftfartstilsynet, Finanstilsynet, Advokatforeningen, Legeforeningen), employers.
- **Mechanism:** For roles where a human signs off on AI-assisted decisions (clinical diagnosis, credit and fraud decisions, legal advice, NAV case decisions, safety engineering):
  - **Periodic unaided proficiency checks.**
  - **AI-off rotations**, for example one session in ten.
  - **Simulation of AI failure**, including seeded errors.
  - **Monitoring of reliance**, such as override rates and rates of disagreement with the AI.

  This follows aviation's recurrent training and the FAA's guidance on keeping manual flying skills.
- **Who pays:** Employers, by building 1–3% of working time into staffing. The cost passes into prices and public budgets. Regulators bear a small standard-setting cost.
- **What makes it stick:** It becomes a licensing and accreditation condition (as with pilots' licences and specialist recertification). Hospital and firm quality indicators include *unaided* performance, not just AI-assisted output.
- **Evidence:** **[E]** skill decay with disuse and automation complacency. **[E/C]** recurrent training maintains performance in aviation. **[C]** the colonoscopy deskilling signal.
- **Main risk:** Box-ticking compliance, a productivity cost, and preserving the *wrong* skills. *Mitigation:* regulators define the *fallback-critical* skill set per profession and review it every three years.
- **[Generalises]** Fully. Licensing exists everywhere, and the EU AI Act's human-oversight requirements for high-risk systems provide a hook.

### I-3. Lifelong Learning and Transition Account, plus a reskilling levy
*Addresses M4, P3 and P6.*

- **Actors:** The state (Lånekassen/NAV/HK-dir), employers, the social partners.
- **Mechanism:** Two parts:
  - **(a)** A personal **learning account** for every adult, topped up each year. Low-skilled workers and those over 50 get extra top-ups. It can only be spent on quality-assured provision (higher vocational colleges, universities, *bransjeprogram*, accredited employer academies).
  - **(b)** A **transition right**: income replacement of up to about 80% of pay (with a cap) for up to one year of full-time study during mid-career, modelled on Sweden's 2022 *omställningsstudiestöd* for ages 27–62.

  Funding includes an **employer reskilling levy** (for example 0.3% of payroll, similar to Sweden's job-security council fees). Employers get it back for documented, certified in-house training.
- **Who pays:** Employers through the levy, the state for top-ups and income replacement, individuals through their time. *Estimate:* **NOK 4–8bn/year** depending on take-up. Part of this is offset by lower spending on disability and long-term benefits.
- **What makes it stick:** The money belongs to the individual and moves with them. The levy aligns employer incentives. The account sits inside the existing *Kompetansereformen* (*Lære hele livet*) and tripartite *bransjeprogram* structures.
- **Evidence:** **[E/C]** Swedish job-security councils show good re-employment rates. **[C]** individual learning accounts have a mixed record: France's CPF suffered fraud and low-value courses, and Singapore's SkillsFuture has low take-up among low-skilled workers.
- **Main risk:** The **Matthew effect**: graduates use the account and low-skilled workers do not. *Mitigation:* outreach through NAV and union learning reps, guidance as a prerequisite, and heavier top-ups for disadvantaged groups.
- **[Generalises]** Moderately. The levy-and-account model works anywhere, but income replacement needs fiscal room.

### I-4. Provider duties: learning modes, honest reliance data, and non-exploitative companionship
*Addresses M1, M3 and M5.*

- **Actors:** AI providers. The state as regulator (through EEA incorporation of the EU AI Act, including Article 4 on AI literacy) and as **purchaser**: the public sector is a huge licence buyer through DFØ/Digdir framework agreements.
- **Mechanism:** Public procurement standards, coordinated at Nordic and EU level, require enterprise AI to provide:
  - A **configurable learning mode** that employers can switch on for trainees: "attempt first", Socratic hints, explanation before answer. Several providers already ship "study" and "learning" modes, so this is achievable.
  - **User-facing reliance insights.** Private data for the individual, such as "you accepted 98% of suggestions in this task category". This must *not* become an employer surveillance tool.
  - **Confidence and provenance signals** that help users calibrate their trust.
  - **For companion and social products:**
    - No engagement-maximising design toward vulnerable or older users.
    - Clear disclosure that the user is talking to an AI.
    - Built-in "bridging" toward human contact.
    - Outcomes measured on users' social connection, not time spent in the app.
- **Who pays:** Providers bear the compliance cost. The state uses its existing purchasing power.
- **What makes it stick:** Procurement conditions plus AI Act enforcement. Nordic joint procurement gives enough market weight that Norway is not a lone rule-maker.
- **Evidence:** **[C]** guardrails in tutoring preserved learning (Bastani et al.). **[C]** heavy companion use was linked to more loneliness (Fang et al. 2025). **[X]** long-run effects of learning modes at work are unknown.
- **Main risk:** Compliance theatre, regulatory capture, and slower adoption that hurts competitiveness. *Mitigation:* outcome-based standards rather than feature checklists, EU-level coordination, and a sunset-and-review clause.

### I-5. The Mentor Corps: phased retirement with a duty to pass knowledge on
*Addresses M1, M4 and M5, plus intergenerational transfer.*

- **Actors:** Employers, NAV/pension policy, municipalities, civil society (volunteer centres, *frivilligsentraler*).
- **Mechanism:**
  - **(a) Senior contracts.** Workers aged 60+ move to reduced hours in a formal mentor role, paired with the professional apprentices from I-1. Norway's flexible pension already allows combining work and pension, and the 2024 pension settlement raises age thresholds, so the timing fits.
  - **(b) Knowledge-transition plans.** Experts in critical roles who are within three years of retirement get a plan based on *working alongside* juniors, not just documentation. AI can help capture explicit knowledge, but tacit knowledge transfers mainly through shared practice.
  - **(c) A stipended civic mentor corps** for retirees and displaced workers: tutoring in schools, supporting newly arrived migrants, mentoring young entrepreneurs.
- **Who pays:** Employers for senior contracts (offset by I-1 mentor credits), municipalities and the state for stipends. *Estimate:* **NOK 0.5–1bn/year**.
- **What makes it stick:** Mentor hours count in collective agreements and pension accrual. Mentoring becomes an *expected* late-career role with real status, not charity.
- **Evidence:** **[C/E]** older volunteers in Experience Corps improved some cognitive and brain measures, and the children they tutored benefited (Carlson et al. 2009, 2015; Fried et al. 2013). **[E]** volunteering is linked to better health in older adults, with partly causal evidence. **[C]** the effect of retirement on cognition ("mental retirement") varies by job complexity (Rohwedder & Willis 2010 vs. later null findings).
- **Main risk:** Tokenism, seniors blocking juniors' progression, or crowding out paid jobs. *Mitigation:* mentor roles are *additional* and time-limited, and juniors evaluate their mentors.

### I-6. Leve hele livet 2.0: a cognitive-reserve package for later life
*Addresses M5. This intervention uses the boring, proven levers.*

- **Actors:** Municipalities (primary health care and *frisklivssentraler*), NAV (assistive devices), GPs, civil society, the health trusts.
- **Mechanism:**
  - Universal **hearing and vision screening** at 60 and 70, with low-friction fitting. NAV already funds hearing aids; the gap is uptake and use.
  - Blood pressure, lipid and physical-activity follow-up.
  - **Social prescribing** through municipalities, linked to *Den kulturelle spaserstokken*, senior centres and learning offers (*folkehøgskole* short courses, senior university).
  - FINGER-style multidomain programmes for high-risk groups.

  **The role of AI:** AI supports detection (hearing screening apps, early warning of loneliness and frailty), logistics and accessibility. It is governed by a "**bridge, not substitute**" rule: municipal AI or welfare-technology deployments must show they *increase* human contact.
- **Who pays:** Municipalities with earmarked state grants. Avoided dementia and care costs partly offset the spending.
- **What makes it stick:** It builds on *Leve hele livet* (the quality reform for older people), puts it in municipal performance agreements, and tracks results through HUNT and SSB data.
- **Evidence:** **[E]** about 45% of dementia cases are linked to 14 modifiable risk factors (Lancet Commission 2024; observational, population-attributable). **[E/C]** multidomain trials: FINGER 2015 and US POINTER 2025 found modest gains. **[C]** hearing aids reduced cognitive decline only in a high-risk subgroup (ACHIEVE 2023). **[C]** commercial brain-training games show little transfer to everyday tasks (Simons et al. 2016), so they should *not* be funded.
- **Main risk:** Medicalising old age, and welfare technology becoming surveillance or a cover for cutting care staff. *Mitigation:* opt-in, data minimisation, and staffing floors that technology cannot replace.

### I-7. Contribution-based security: making non-market contribution count
*Addresses M4 and P5. This is more of a contingency plan than an immediate programme.*

- **Actors:** The state, social partners, civil society.
- **Mechanism:** Prepare, without yet triggering, mechanisms that recognise care, mentoring and civic roles:
  - Pension accrual for documented care and mentoring (Norway already credits some care work).
  - "Participation income" pilots for people whose work has been displaced.
  - Status markers: credentials and public recognition.

  The trigger is pre-agreed labour-market indicators, for example sustained falls in employment of young adults in exposed occupations.
- **Who pays:** The state. This is the most expensive option if triggered, so it is held in reserve.
- **What makes it stick:** Agreeing the triggers *now* through cross-party consensus, in the way the pension settlements are made.
- **Evidence:** **[C]** Finland's 2017–18 basic income experiment found small employment effects and better well-being. **[E]** unemployment harms well-being beyond lost income. **[X]** whether contribution roles can replace work's "latent functions".
- **Main risk:** Weaker work incentives, fiscal cost, and contribution roles seen as second-class. The case for preparing the triggers now: deciding in a crisis produces worse design.

## 4. The contract (ages 22 to end of life)

| Party | **Owes** | **Receives** |
|---|---|---|
| **Individual** | Effort to keep load-bearing skills (supervisory, fallback, constitutive); honest use of learning modes during training; passing knowledge on in late career; looking after sensory and physical health | A portable learning account and transition right; a first job that teaches; recognition of non-market contribution; AI tools that respect agency; the right to offload outside safety-critical roles |
| **Family / close network** | Sharing care so that midlife carers can keep learning; staying in contact with older relatives (AI as a bridge, not a replacement); modelling deliberate AI use for children | Caregiver leave and pension credits; municipal support; honest companion-AI products; flexible work |
| **Employers / educators (incl. unions)** | Hiring and *training* juniors (I-1); protected learning time; "manual hours" in high-stakes roles (I-2); the reskilling levy (I-3); knowledge-transition plans (I-5); worker involvement when AI is introduced (*Hovedavtalen*, technology chapter) | Apprenticeship grants and levy rebates; a reliable pipeline of experts; mentor-time credits; lower liability from documented supervisory competence; a workforce that can catch AI failures |
| **State (national and municipal)** | Funding accounts, apprenticeship grants and transition income; setting competence standards through regulators; procurement standards for AI; the later-life health package (I-6); pre-agreed triggers for contribution security (I-7); a measurement system | A resilient, adaptable workforce; lower spending on disability, long-term benefits and dementia care; democratic resilience; preserved tax capacity |
| **AI providers** | Learning modes; private reliance insights for users; calibration signals; non-exploitative companion design; outcome data for research (privacy-preserving); meeting Nordic/EU procurement conditions | Access to large public procurement; regulatory predictability; trust and legitimacy; long-term demand from skilled users who can judge AI output |

## 5. Measurement: how we would know it is working

Principles: measure **distributions, not averages** (who falls behind?), measure **unaided as well as AI-assisted performance**, and keep counter-indicators so we do not buy capability at an unseen cost. Build on existing Norwegian infrastructure: SSB registers and *a-ordningen*, the *Lærevilkårsmonitoren*, the *Livskvalitetsundersøkelsen*, HUNT, and PIAAC.

| Domain | Indicator | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Junior pipeline (M1)** | Junior hiring ratio (ages 22–29 as a share of new hires) in AI-exposed occupations; share of juniors in structured training; time to independent competence; retention after two years | *a-ordningen* registers; I-1 monitoring |
| **Supervisory competence (M2)** | Unaided proficiency compared with baseline (e.g. adenoma detection without AI; accuracy in AI-off case decisions); **detection rate for seeded AI errors** | Regulators; I-2 audits |
| **Judgment and calibration (M3)** | Calibration (confidence versus accuracy) on judgment tasks; ability to spot AI errors and unsupported claims; diversity of solutions in team tasks | New national **capability panel** (see below) |
| **Adult skills** | PIAAC literacy, numeracy and adaptive problem-solving, **with a gap measure by education, age and immigrant background**; an annual short-form national sample between PIAAC rounds | OECD, SSB |
| **Learning equity (M4)** | Participation in learning by skill level and age; account take-up by low-skilled workers; re-employment rate and wage after displacement | *Lærevilkårsmonitoren*, NAV |
| **Agency and meaning** | Self-efficacy; sense of meaning and purpose; perceived control over one's own work and AI use | *Livskvalitetsundersøkelsen* (already includes meaning items) |
| **Late life (M5)** | Age-specific dementia incidence; hearing-aid *use* (not just ownership); loneliness prevalence; frequency of social contact; healthy life years | HUNT, SSB, NAV, municipal data |
| **Intergenerational transfer** | Share of retiring experts with completed transition plans; mentor hours; mentors' and mentees' assessments | I-5 monitoring |
| **AI reliance** | Aggregated, privacy-preserving acceptance and override rates by task type | Provider reporting under I-4 |
| **Counter-indicators** | Productivity, the employment rate, the cost per apprentice, and user satisfaction | National accounts, surveys |

**New infrastructure:** a **National Capability Panel**: a longitudinal sample, for example 10,000 adults followed from age 22, assessed every two years on unaided and AI-assisted tasks, calibration, and agency. It would link to the 0–22 stage's assessment so that we follow the same people across the handoff.

**Goodhart guard:** indicators inform policy and are not targets for individual employers, except where a licensing standard requires them (I-2).

## 6. Handoff interface

### 6.1 What I assume people bring at about 22–25

*The 0–22 stage should confirm or revise each of these.*

| # | Assumed capability | Why my proposals depend on it |
|---|---|---|
| A1 | **Unaided foundational skills:** functional literacy and numeracy at about PIAAC level 3 *without* AI, and the ability to reason in writing for about an hour without assistance | I-1 and I-2 build *supervisory* competence on top of domain basics. Without these foundations, apprenticeships turn into remedial schooling |
| A2 | **At least one experience of mastering something hard** (sport, music, craft, a subject) through sustained, productive struggle | I-1's "attempt first" rules depend on tolerance for difficulty. Without it, juniors will route around the scaffolds |
| A3 | **AI-calibrated judgment:** can check sources, spot confident errors, and knows when *not* to trust a tool | The core of M3 protection. I-4's calibration signals help only those who already understand calibration |
| A4 | **Metacognition and a learning identity:** knows how to learn, and expects to keep learning | I-3 accounts depend on *self-directed* learning. If this is weak, take-up skews toward the already-advantaged and the Matthew effect grows |
| A5 | **Social and collaborative experience in person:** team work, conflict, feedback | Apprenticeship and mentoring run on relationships. Losing this capacity makes I-1 and I-5 harder |
| A6 | **Attention control:** can sustain focus on a single task | A precondition for deep skill-building in every intervention |
| A7 | **A portable learning record** (format agreed with the 0–22 stage) | Lets I-1 and I-3 build on what people already know instead of repeating it |

**If the 0–22 stage cannot deliver A1–A4 for most young people**, my stage needs a **bridging year**, for example a *folkehøgskole*-style or vocational foundation year funded under I-3. This raises costs significantly. **This is the most important dependency between our two proposals.**

### 6.2 What my stage passes back upstream

The adults in my stage *are* the families, teachers and mentors in the 0–22 contract. So the handoff runs in both directions.

- **Parents:** Adult AI habits shape children's habits. I-2 and I-4 norms (deliberate, calibrated use) should spread into homes. Flexible work and caregiver leave give parents time.
- **Teachers:** Teachers are adult workers too. I-2-style "manual hours" and I-3 accounts apply to them, which keeps the people teaching children's skills sharp themselves.
- **Labour-market signals:** A shared signal about which skills are load-bearing (from I-2's fallback-critical skill lists) should feed back into curricula.
- **The Mentor Corps (I-5)** brings retirees into schools as tutors (Experience Corps model), which benefits both ends of the life course.

### 6.3 Proposed joint commitments with the 0–22 stage

1. A **shared assessment framework** (unaided task, AI-assisted task, error detection, calibration) used at around 18, 25 and 35.
2. A **shared learning-record format** that moves across the handoff.
3. A **shared principle P1** (scaffold → spar → prosthesis), so the two halves read as one life-course logic.

## 7. The pilot: AI-era Professional Apprenticeship trial

**Question:** For juniors in AI-intensive knowledge work, does a structured scaffolding protocol with senior mentors produce better *unaided* competence and judgment at 12 months, without an unacceptable loss of productivity?

**Design:** A pre-registered cluster-randomised trial (randomised by team or office to avoid contamination), starting within 12 months.

- **Sites:** 3–4 sectors with willing partners, for example:
  - one municipality or NAV office (case work),
  - one health trust (clinical documentation or radiology support),
  - one large law, accounting or consulting firm,
  - one software or engineering employer.
- **Participants:** About 400–600 new graduate hires.
- **Arm A (control):** Normal AI-enabled onboarding.
- **Arm B (treatment):**
  - AI learning mode switched on (I-4).
  - "Attempt first" protocol.
  - Weekly AI-off practice with feedback.
  - 10–20% protected learning time.
  - A named mentor, drawing where possible on workers aged 60+ on senior contracts (I-5).

**Outcomes at 6 and 12 months:**
- *Primary:* unaided performance on a standardised work sample; **detection rate for seeded AI errors**.
- *Secondary:* AI-assisted productivity and quality; time to independent competence; calibration; self-efficacy and meaning; two-year retention.
- *Mentor side:* mentors' well-being, cognitive engagement and intention to keep working.

**Governance and funding:**
- *Funding:* Forskningsrådet / HK-dir with matching funds from employers.
- *Oversight:* the tripartite partners sit on the steering board.
- *Evaluation:* an independent academic team (for example Frisch Centre / OsloMet / NTNU).
- *Rough cost:* **NOK 15–25 million** over 24 months.

**Why this pilot:**
1. It tests the **central contested claim** for my stage: does AI speed up or hollow out the path to expertise?
2. It tests three interventions at once (I-1, I-4, I-5).
3. It produces exactly the indicators in Section 5.
4. A null result would be very informative. If juniors with ordinary AI access build expertise just as well, much of this proposal should shrink. **I would rather find that out early.**

**Runner-up pilot:** a municipal RCT of a "bridge, not substitute" AI companion for isolated adults aged 75+, measured on human contact and loneliness rather than engagement.

## 8. Open questions and biggest uncertainties

1. **Does AI speed up or hollow out expertise?** The steelman (instant feedback, a tutor for every junior) could win. The evidence points both ways (Brynjolfsson et al. 2025 on lasting gains versus Bastani et al. 2025 on harm to learning). *The pilot is designed to answer this.*
2. **Which skills are load-bearing, and for how long?** If AI comes to supervise AI reliably, the case for human supervisory competence weakens. Fallback and constitutive skills would remain. We need a dynamic, profession-by-profession review rather than a fixed list.
3. **Timing and size of labour-market effects.** Nordic register data show close to zero effects so far (Humlum & Vestergaard 2025). US signals for young workers are disputed. I-7 is deliberately held in reserve until triggered, and I could be wrong in either direction.
4. **The normative foundation.** Do we justify these interventions by productivity and safety, or by flourishing as valuable in itself? If AI makes human skill economically unnecessary, only the second justification survives. The merged proposal should say plainly which one we rely on, because the answer decides how much paternalism and spending can be justified.
5. **AI companionship in old age.** Long-run effects are unknown. Short-run studies conflict (reduced loneliness in brief experiments, more loneliness with heavy use). We must not let the lack of evidence become a reason to cut care staff.
6. **What is causing the Flynn reversal?** It began before AI. If its causes (education content, reading habits, test motivation) are still at work, AI may speed up or reverse a trend we do not understand.
7. **Measuring reliance without surveillance.** User-facing reliance data is useful but easily misused by employers. The governance of this data is unsolved.
8. **Competitiveness and acting alone.** The friction we introduce costs productivity in the short run. Acting at Nordic or EU level reduces the cost but slows action. The likely trade-off (some short-term productivity for long-term resilience) is a political choice, not a technical one.
9. **Political economy.** Future junior cohorts and future dementia patients have no voice in today's bargaining. Tripartite systems protect insiders. Who speaks for the missing rung?
10. **[Generalises]?** The proposal leans heavily on Nordic assets: high trust, strong social partners, register data, a fiscal buffer and an apprenticeship tradition. In liberal market economies, the levers move toward regulation and tax credits, and the equity risk is greater.
